The ask

Proposal for agency and research partners

Every trout, every catch, on the record.

Colorado has roughly a million fishing licences in circulation and surveys most of its small streams about once a decade. This is a proposal to close that gap by turning routine angling into a continuous, individually identified fish record, gathered at no cost to the fish and at a fraction of the cost of a survey crew.

The monitoring gap

A decade between snapshots.

Standardised electrofishing surveys are the backbone of fisheries management and they are expensive, so smaller streams are sampled on a cycle measured in years. Between those visits, the record of what is happening in a reach is close to empty, while thousands of anglers stand in it every season holding the exact information a biologist would want.

Did the stocking take?

Survival, dispersal and growth of stocked fish are direct management questions, and today they are answered by inference rather than by following the individuals.

Where do fish move?

Movement between reaches, into and out of tributaries, and in response to flow or temperature, at an individual level.

How fast do they grow?

Growth measured on the same fish across seasons rather than inferred from a length-frequency distribution.

Is the trend real?

Continuous recapture data across many reaches is what separates a bad year from a decline.

Method

The fish is already carrying its tag.

Natural body patterns are individually unique and stable over years. Identification from a photograph is an established method in the literature, not a claim we are making for the first time, and it is not confined to spotted fish: published work identifies individual common carp from scale pattern and Eurasian perch from stripes, alongside the salmonid work below.

"One of most frequently used methods involves capturing and tagging fish. However, these processes have been reported to cause tissue damage, premature tag loss, and decreased swimming capacity."
Photo Identification of Individual Salmo trutta Based on Deep Learning, Applied Sciences 11(19):9039, 2021. doi:10.3390/app11199039

The welfare case and the sample-size case point the same way. A method that requires no capture beyond the angling already happening, and no implant, can be applied to every fish landed rather than to the handful a crew can process.

"In both populations, all annotated images ranked the first ranking score, indicating perfect recognition. The long-term analysis revealed a high performance in recognition of individuals because 75% of the images of fish at the age of 2 years were successfully matched with images of the same fish at the age of 3 years."
Colihueque N, Estay F (2025), rainbow trout Oncorhynchus mykiss, wild (n=56) and farmed (n=45) populations. Acta Ichthyologica et Piscatoria 55: 181–190. doi:10.3897/aiep.55.151044

The pattern persists across a year of growth. That study used I3S, an interactive identification system applied to whale sharks and sea turtles for two decades, which requires no training data. Our matcher uses the same class of method first, because it can be validated against the tens of fish a pilot actually produces.

"We proved that the methodology can be used as a non-invasive substitute for invasive fish tagging. The methodology can be adapted to any fish species with dot skin patterns."
Atlantic salmon (Salmo salar) identified by dot skin pattern, 328 individuals at 100% accuracy, with pattern stability confirmed over six months. Scientific Reports 11, 2021. s41598-021-96476-4

Substitution holds at population scale under controlled photography. Controlled photography is the binding condition, and it is the part this project actually contributes: moving the experimental control onto a phone held by an angler.

What the app contributes

  • A guided capture overlay that fixes pose, orientation and framing, and refuses a photograph that is too small in frame, too angled, or out of focus, while the fish is still in the net
  • A burst of frames per encounter, so within-individual variation in wetness, light and pose is captured in a single handling of about thirty seconds
  • Length against a consistent in-frame reference, so growth is comparable between observers
  • Automatic attachment of USGS discharge and water temperature and National Weather Service conditions to every record
  • Ranked candidate matches confirmed by a person, never assigned automatically

A Colorado case

Greenback cutthroat recovery is the hardest thing to monitor and the easiest thing to photograph.

Colorado's state fish is federally threatened, and recovery depends on tracking small reintroduced populations in headwater streams that are expensive to reach and costly to survey. Cutthroat are also among the most heavily and distinctively spotted salmonids, which is precisely the condition under which photographic identification performs best.

A reintroduced population monitored by the anglers and volunteers who already walk those drainages would produce individual survival and growth data between formal surveys, without a single fish being handled for a tag.

There is precedent for exactly this species group. Gifford and Mayhood catalogued natural marks for identifying individual fish in small at-risk populations of westslope cutthroat, and a long-term study comparing datasets from 1997 and 1999 identified adult cutthroat from skin spots after two years.

We are not proposing to replace recovery monitoring. We are proposing to fill the years between visits with individually identified observations that currently do not exist.

Limitations, stated plainly

What this data is not.

A proposal that only lists strengths is not a proposal a reviewer should trust. These are the constraints we would want a statistician to know before the first record is collected.

Angler catch is biased

Catch is skewed toward catchable size classes, toward the species anglers pursue, and toward water people can reach. Any legal method counts, so this is not a fly-fishing dataset, but it still cannot produce an unbiased population estimate and is not a substitute for standardised electrofishing.

Effort is uneven

More data arrives on weekends, in good weather, and near access points. Effort has to be modelled explicitly rather than assumed uniform, which is why every record carries time, location and conditions.

Field accuracy is still being measured

Published accuracy comes from controlled photography. Nothing in the literature was validated on photographs taken one-handed in a river, so we are measuring it: a field trial has been running on a Colorado River tailwater reach since mid-2026 and is ongoing. That number is a deliverable of this work, not an assumption behind it.

Performance varies by species

The method is not limited to spotted fish: individual identification is published for common carp from scale pattern and for Eurasian perch from stripes. But every species needs its own validation. Rainbow spot patterns are finer and denser than brown, so accuracy should be expected to differ, and each new species starts at zero until it is measured.

How accuracy gets established. PIT-tagged fish provide hard ground truth: a tagged fish photographed twice is one labelled pair. Performance is reported as rank-1 accuracy, top-5, and false acceptance rate, each against the chance baseline for the gallery size, with the gallery padded to simulate larger populations. The programme proceeds to public release only if false acceptance can be driven near zero without collapsing recall. A conservative "no match" is scientifically usable. A confident wrong match silently corrupts a growth series, which is the outcome worth spending money to avoid.

Two deployment models

Agency instrument first, public platform second.

Model A. Agency and research use

Accounts issued to biologists, technicians and permitted volunteers on defined study reaches. Precise coordinates retained. Emphasis on ground truth, matcher validation and controlled comparison against electrofishing on the same water.

  • A field trial has been running on a Colorado River tailwater reach since mid-2026 and is ongoing
  • Three study reaches in year one
  • PIT ground truth on every study reach
  • Validation report published regardless of outcome

Model B. Open public release

Free to any Colorado angler. Every catch photographed through the guide becomes a candidate record. Location published at reach resolution rather than as a pin, so the dataset does not become a map of where the fish are holding.

  • Statewide, no licence beyond a fishing licence
  • Volunteer coordinators with reference equipment
  • Open data release on a fixed schedule

Economics at scale

The anglers have already paid for the fieldwork.

This is the part that makes the case. Almost all the cost is fixed staff and infrastructure, while the data collection is done by people who bought a licence for the privilege of doing it. Every additional participant lowers the cost per record, and the state pays nothing for the labour.

What the same record would cost the state to collect

A fisheries technician at $55,000 plus 25% fringe costs about $68,750 a year fully loaded, before a vehicle, a boat, an electrofisher or a permit. How many fish one technician can capture, measure, photograph and tag in a field day varies enormously with stream size, density and crew configuration, so rather than assert a single rate we have shown the whole plausible range. The conclusion does not depend on where in that range the truth sits.

Even at the most generous assumption, agency staff time alone costs twenty times more per fish than this programme, and that excludes equipment, vehicles, permitting and overtime. For scale, a published winter survival study PIT-tagged 393 fish in a single autumn electrofishing campaign on one 550 m reach with a two-person team (Watz et al., Ecology and Evolution).

The deeper asymmetry is recapture, not tagging. A PIT tag produces no data at all until that particular fish is caught again by someone carrying a reader, and first-pass electrofishing capture efficiency has been measured at around 46% for salmonids (USFS, Montana streams), with catchability of 0.23 for backpack units against 0.64 for towed barges. Recapture is the expensive half of mark-recapture. Anglers recapture fish continuously, all season, across every accessible reach, at no marginal cost to the agency.

These comparisons are deliberately like for like on the photographic record only. They are not a claim that this replaces standardised survey work, which answers different questions. They are a claim that no agency budget could buy this volume of individually identified observations any other way, and that the licence revenue already being collected is what makes it possible.

Funding pathways

Where we think this fits, including where it does not.

Sport Fish Restoration

The closest fit. Dingell-Johnson and Wallop-Breaux excise revenue, apportioned to state agencies, supports fisheries research, management and aquatic education, at a 75 to 25 federal to non-federal match. This is angler money returned to angler data.

State Wildlife Grants

Applicable where the target is a Species of Greatest Conservation Need. Greenback cutthroat monitoring is the obvious candidate.

Citizen science funds

The federal Citizen Science Fund is a strong thematic match at smaller scale and requires a partnership with a federal unit. NSF informal STEM learning programmes fund public participation in research at programme scale.

What we are not asking for. No exclusivity, no ownership of the resulting data, and no requirement that any agency adopt the software. If the validation fails, the finding is published and the programme stops.

Open data

Public money, public record.

  • Records released under an open licence on a fixed schedule, in a documented schema, with a stable API
  • Public waters published at full spatial resolution; private waters published at reach resolution only, with the landowner's participation being voluntary
  • Photographs retained as the evidentiary record, with the matching pipeline versioned so any historical identification can be re-run and audited
  • Negative results published. If field accuracy does not clear the bar, that number is the deliverable

Why location needs care

A public dataset of exactly where large trout are caught is also a map for anyone who wants to remove them. Spatial precision is a conservation decision, not a technical default, and it is why private participation is at reach resolution and why precise coordinates stay with the managing agency.
